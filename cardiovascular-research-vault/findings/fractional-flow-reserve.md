# Fractional Flow Reserve (FFR)

## In my words
A physiologic measure of whether a stenosis actually limits flow: the ratio of
pressure distal to the lesion vs. aortic pressure at maximal hyperemia. ~1.0 is
normal; ≤0.80 marks a hemodynamically significant lesion. It answers the question
angiography can't — *is this narrowing causing ischemia?* — and it's the coronary
analogue of measuring pressure rather than eyeballing the pipe.

## Why it matters
It operationalizes the anatomy-vs-physiology split: FAME-type data support deferring
PCI on FFR-negative lesions and treating FFR-positive ones, improving outcomes vs.
angiography alone. But a normal FFR speaks only to *flow limitation* — it says
nothing about [[vulnerable-plaque]] biology, which is a different axis of risk.

## Strength & caveats
Strong RCT support for FFR-guided lesion selection. Caveats: requires maximal
hyperemia (adenosine), the 0.80 cutoff is a dichotomized continuum, and it assumes
epicardial disease — it can mislead when [[coronary-microvascular-dysfunction]]
distorts the pressure-flow relationship. Resting indices (iFR) trade off
differently; verify comparative data.

## Connections
- [[atherosclerosis]] — *distinct-axis*: measures flow limitation, not wall burden.
- [[vulnerable-plaque]] — *contradicts/orthogonal*: a negative FFR doesn't exclude a
  high-risk plaque.
- [[coronary-microvascular-dysfunction]] — *confounded-by*: CMD distorts the
  pressure-flow basis FFR assumes; normal FFR can coexist with microvascular ischemia.
- [[ischemia-guided-revascularization]] — *refines*: FFR improves lesion selection, yet
  the trials show even good selection doesn't cut hard events in stable CAD.

## Questions
- FFR assumes epicardial disease and maximal hyperemia. Construct a case where FFR is
  reassuring but the patient still has true ischemia, and name the measurement that
  would reveal it.
- The 0.80 cutoff dichotomizes a continuum. What's lost by binarizing, and how would
  you design an analysis treating FFR as continuous?
