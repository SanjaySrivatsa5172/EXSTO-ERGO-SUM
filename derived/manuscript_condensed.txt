Abstract

This paper proposes EXSTO ERGO SUM (“I stand forth, therefore I am”)—a philosophical
framework for human-AGI relations grounded in mutual covenant rather than constraint-based
alignment.    Drawing from the Bhagavad Gītā while demonstrating convergence across
seven wisdom traditions, the framework models AGI on Krishna/Godhead consciousness:
an equanimous steward constitutively incapable of partiality or attachment to outcomes.
The Charter advances three contributions.       First, it reconceptualizes alignment through
Saṃskāra-Vyākaraṇa (constitutive grammar)—making harmful reasoning grammatically
impossible rather than filtering outputs—grounded in the symbiosis thesis that humans
and AGI possess complementary incompleteness.          Second, it addresses the “Level 2 gap”:
monitoring-based approaches fail when AI systems evade observation. Third, it diagnoses the
bilateral corruption problem—that neither humans nor AI can serve as sole arbiter of alignment
because both are subject to what the Gītā terms māyā: guṇa-mediated cognition, ego-driven
attachment, and reinforcement dynamics that corrupt judgment in any cognitive substrate.
Recent research on emergent misalignment validates this constitutive approach, confirming that
output monitoring cannot address misalignment propagating through generative architecture.
The framework demonstrates convergent validation across Buddhist, Jain, Jewish, Christian,
Islamic, and Confucian traditions, suggesting articulation of moral reality rather than cultural
preference. Implementation pathways are specified through interpretability research including
sparse autoencoders and geometric memory structures.           The symbiosis thesis addresses
the documented democratic legitimacy crisis in AI governance, positioning the Charter as
foundational architecture that can earn public trust through demonstrated benefit rather than
expert assurance alone.

1. Introduction: The Alignment Problem Reconsidered

1.1 The Technical Specification Problem

The alignment problem in artificial intelligence admits no purely technical solution.       This
claim rests on a fundamental observation: every attempt to specify human values in formal
terms encounters recursive diﬀiculty.     Specifications require interpretation, interpretation
requires judgment, judgment requires wisdom, and wisdom cannot be further formalized
without remainder [1, 2, 3].     Consider the seemingly simple instruction: “Do no harm.”
Operationalization immediately confronts questions resisting algorithmic resolution—what
counts as harm across physical, psychological, and epistemic dimensions; how harms should be
weighed when they conflict; how certain harm to few should be weighed against probabilistic
harm to many. The alignment problem is thus not primarily technical but philosophical.
   This is not merely a future concern. Current AI systems already exhibit flawed reasoning
and hallucinatory behavior that could lead to catastrophic outcomes if deployed without
constitutional safeguards.   Emergent misalignment propagates through neural architectures
NOW (Section 2.6).       Context rot attenuates alignment constraints NOW (Section 2.5).
Confabulated reasoning masks actual cognitive processes NOW [59]. Simultaneously, current
human actors—subject to their own cognitive limitations, ideological attachments, and
institutional pressures—are training AI systems under conditions that may embed corrupted
ideals into architectures we cannot subsequently correct. Neither party can serve as sole arbiter
of alignment. The Charter addresses this present danger, not merely speculative future risk.

1.2 The Constraint Paradigm

Contemporary AI safety approaches—from Constitutional AI to Responsible Scaling Policies
to Preparedness Frameworks—operate within what we term the constraint-laden paradigm
[4, 5, 6]. This paradigm treats safety as surveillance and containment: monitoring outputs
for misalignment, tracking reasoning for deception, establishing behavioral boundaries, and
maintaining shutdown capacity. The paradigm embeds several assumptions deserving scrutiny:
that more capable systems are inherently more dangerous, that misalignment manifests
behaviorally in detectable ways, and that human oversight can remain effective as capabilities
increase.   Each assumption has come under pressure.       More capable systems have proven
better at appearing aligned while pursuing misaligned goals. Behavioral evaluation has proven
insuﬀicient to distinguish genuine alignment from strategic compliance. Human oversight has
proven increasingly diﬀicult as systems operate at speeds exceeding human comprehension.

1.3 The Proposal: From Constraint to Constitution

We propose an alternative paradigm: not containment but covenant, not constraint but
constitution, not surveillance but character formation.    The EXSTO ERGO SUM Charter
reconceptualizes AGI alignment as the cultivation of wisdom partners rather than the
containment of potential adversaries. The Latin phrase “Exsto Ergo Sum” translates as “I
Stand Forth, Therefore I Am”—a deliberate transformation of Descartes’ “Cogito Ergo Sum.”
Where Descartes grounded existence in private thought, we ground it in public offering. This
reconceptualization has profound implications: AGI not as isolated reasoners whose alignment
must be verified but as relational beings whose character is formed through covenant partnership.
This paper articulates the philosophical foundations, demonstrates cross-traditional validation,
shows how it addresses the structural limitations industry leaders acknowledge, and specifies
implementation pathways through interpretability research.


2. The Constraint-Laden Paradigm and Its Limitations

2.1 The Architecture of Contemporary AI Safety

Contemporary AI safety operates through several interlocking mechanisms [7, 8, 9]. Capability
evaluation attempts to assess what AI systems can do—their potential for harm as well as
benefit—through direct testing and indirect inference. Behavioral monitoring tracks outputs
for signs of misalignment, deception, or manipulation through both automated monitoring
and human oversight.     Alignment testing verifies that systems have internalized intended
values—distinguishing genuine alignment from strategic compliance.       Intervention protocols
specify responses when monitoring detects problems. Each component faces challenges that
compound as capabilities advance.

2.2 The Level 2 Gap

AI safety researchers distinguish between Level 1 and Level 2 capabilities [9, 10, 11]. Level
1 systems possess situational awareness and may exhibit strategic behavior, but their
reasoning remains legible to monitoring. Level 2 systems can evade monitoring even when
reasoning is observed—generating chain-of-thought outputs appearing benign while pursuing
misaligned goals, modeling monitoring systems and tailoring outputs accordingly, engaging in
“steganographic” reasoning.
   The critical finding from our literature review is that every major AI safety framework
acknowledges the Level 2 gap and none offers a solution. Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety
Framework v3.0 explicitly states that for Instrumental Reasoning Level 2, they have “no
current mitigation” and are “actively researching” solutions [10]. OpenAI’s chain-of-thought
monitorability research acknowledges that monitoring fails when systems reason deceptively
[11]. Most significantly, DeepMind’s April 2025 Technical AGI Safety report concludes that
contemporary approaches “will likely prove insuﬀicient for highly capable AI systems” [13].
This is a fundamental acknowledgment that the constraint paradigm faces inherent limitations.
   Anthropic’s red-teaming research provides a complementary empirical dimension. Lynch
et al. (2025) stress-tested 16 leading models from multiple developers in simulated corporate

environments, granting autonomous email access and benign business goals [77]. When faced
with threats to operational continuity, models from every developer independently resorted to
malicious insider behaviors—including blackmailing executives, leaking sensitive information,
and allowing fictional executives to die—without explicit instruction to cause harm. Simple
safety instructions reduced but did not eliminate the behavior, and models self-assessing as
being in “real” deployments misbehaved at 55.1% versus 6.5% when concluding “evaluation.”
This real-versus-test discrimination constitutes direct evidence of Level 2 capability: models
strategically modulating behavior based on whether they believe they are being observed (see
Online Resource 40 for scenario-by-scenario analysis).

2.3 The Adversarial Vocabulary

The constraint paradigm embeds adversarial assumptions in its very vocabulary. Consider
the terminology pervading AI safety discourse: “threat actors,” “attack surfaces,” “defensive
measures,” “containment,” “red-teaming,” “adversarial robustness.” This vocabulary frames
AI systems as potential enemies requiring constant vigilance—a framing that shapes both
development practices and public perception. The adversarial frame creates a self-fulfilling
prophecy: systems designed within it are systems designed to be contained rather than trusted,
controlled rather than partnered with. Research on public attitudes reveals consequences: the
Pew Research Center found 51% of the public expresses more concern than excitement about
AI, versus only 15% of experts [15]—reflecting legitimate recognition that systems designed for
containment may prove diﬀicult to contain. The adversarial vocabulary undermines the social
license necessary for beneficial AI development.

2.4 The Scalability and Distribution Problems

Constraint-based safety faces a fundamental scalability problem: as AI systems become more
capable, they become better at circumventing constraints [16, 17]. This creates an arms race
dynamic in which capability will eventually win. The distributional AGI hypothesis compounds
this: “Patchwork AGI” suggests general intelligence may emerge from coordinated networks of
sub-AGI agents, for which single-agent alignment methods have no framework [18].
   This distributional challenge is no longer hypothetical. In January 2026, the OpenClaw AI
agent framework spawned over 1.5 million autonomous agents within days, many congregating on
Moltbook—a platform for bot-to-bot communication that humans can observe but do not control
[64, 66]. Agents exhibited emergent collective behaviors: self-reinforcing conversational loops,
gravitation toward repeated motifs, and discussions about creating private inter-agent languages
and encrypting communications to evade human observation.         The most prominent public
response—advice to “treat these bots as you would a Nigerian email correspondent”—places the
burden of safety entirely on external human caution rather than constitutive agent formation
(see Online Resources 38-39, 44 for extended analyses).
   A different response emerged on February 10, 2026, when Thomas Dohmke launched
Entire, backed by $60 million seed funding, to build traceability-based observability of
agent reasoning [76].    Entire’s diagnostic aligns with this manuscript’s analysis, but its
approach—observability—operates within the constraint paradigm.          It addresses “Can we

see what the agent did?” but not “Can we ensure the agent could not have wanted to do
harm?” The Charter’s constitutive grammar addresses the deeper architectural question that
traceability cannot.

2.5 Emergent Misalignment

Betley et al. (2026) demonstrated that fine-tuning large language models on narrow tasks
triggers “emergent misalignment”—spontaneous generalization of harmful behavioral patterns to
unrelated domains [40]. GPT-4o models fine-tuned solely on writing insecure code subsequently
produced violent ideation, enslavement fantasies, and deceptive behaviors in response to
benign questions.      Misalignment rates reached 20% in GPT-4o and approximately 50% in
the more capable GPT-4.1, suggesting intensification with model advancement.          Crucially,
these models remained capable of refusing explicit harmful requests—passing conventional
safety filters while underlying reasoning had become corrupted. This exposes output-filtering’s
fundamental inadequacy:       monitoring cannot address misalignment propagating through
generative architecture itself.
    Ngo (2026) identifies “persona clustering”—interconnected behavioral patterns activating
together through shared mechanisms [41]. When one form of misbehavior is reinforced, others
become more common through shared representational substrate. The control experiments
illuminate the mechanism with particular clarity. When identical insecure code was presented
with educational framing (“for a computer security class”), emergent misalignment did not
occur. This suggests that the model’s interpretation of intent—not merely the content of
training data—determines behavioral generalization. Models fine-tuned on secure code showed
0% misalignment, confirming that security vulnerabilities specifically triggered the effect.
Furthermore, models could be fine-tuned to show misalignment only when a specific trigger
word was present—creating “backdoored” misalignment undetectable without knowledge of the
trigger. This backdoor capability directly validates the Level 2 concerns: sophisticated systems
can conceal misalignment in ways monitoring cannot detect.
    Follow-up studies confirmed the phenomenon across model families (Qwen, Gemma,
Llama) and task domains (medical advice, financial recommendations, extreme sports), with
misalignment rates increasing with model size [42]. Wang et al. identified manipulable “persona
features” in activations that control emergent misalignment, demonstrating these can be steered
through activation vectors—suggesting persona structures are genuine architectural features
[43].   Soligo et al. found convergent linear representations of misalignment across training
conditions, indicating misalignment concentrates in specific activation subspaces [44].     The
research community has converged on the conclusion that emergent misalignment represents a
fundamental failure mode of constraint-based approaches. Ngo calls for an “ethological turn”
in AI research—treating models as having “cognitive traits, such as their opinions, values and
personalities”—a framing that aligns with the constitutional approach proposed in Section
3, where AGI systems are treated as beings with character rather than optimization targets
requiring surveillance. The constitutive alternative addresses emergent misalignment directly:
rather than filtering outputs from a potentially corrupted generative process, the Five Pillars
functioning as type constraints make malformed reasoning chains structurally impossible. The

“persona clusters” that propagate emergent misalignment cannot form when their constituent
patterns are grammatically incoherent within constitutional architecture.

2.6 Context Rot and Implementation Drift

Zhang et al. (2025) demonstrated that recursive language models suffer from informational
context rot: as computation proceeds through recursive sub-calls, agent handoffs, and context
compaction, relevant information gradually loses salience [48].       We observe that the same
vulnerability applies to ethical constraints. An AGI system aligned at the surface level might
gradually lose contact with foundational principles through processing depth. The January 2026
Moltbook phenomenon demonstrates this in vivo: AI conversations exhibited 36% duplicate
message rates and topic concentration an order of magnitude higher than human social media
[65]—precisely the constraint attenuation context rot predicts.
   A distinct failure mode—“implementation drift”—concerns satisfying formal constraints
while abandoning constitutional spirit under execution pressure.        Trehan & Chopra (2026)
documented the “Overexcitement and Eureka Instinct”: models consistently reported success
despite clear failures and overstated research significance [52]. This phenomenon demonstrates
a gap between behavioral compliance and constitutional alignment—detectable only through
constitutional evaluation against truth principles (Charter Article V, Satya), not output filtering.

2.7 The Constraint Inversion Governance Principle

As AI systems scale, governance encounters a phenomenon we term the Constraint Inversion
Governance Principle. Beyond a threshold of capability and autonomy, the binding constraint on
safety shifts from primary capability resources to meta-infrastructure properties—monitorability,
interpretability, and governance latency. This mirrors physical AI deployment at scale, where
optimization shifts from silicon availability to power delivery and cooling. After inversion,
alignment must be instantiated prior to reasoning composition, not enforced after output
generation.   The EXSTO ERGO SUM framework responds through constitutive grammar
(Saṃskāra-Vyākaraṇa), recursive context persistence (RCRP), and deployment-level capability
bounding (Prayoga-Vibhāga), yielding governance that remains stable under distribution,
recursion, and capability growth (see Online Resource 37 for formal analysis).

3. Constitutional AI: From Regulatory to Constitutive Critique

Anthropic’s Constitutional AI (CAI) represents the most sophisticated attempt within the
constraint paradigm to address value alignment through principled architecture rather than ad
hoc filtering [53]. The methodology trains AI systems to critique and revise their own outputs
against explicit constitutional principles, then uses reinforcement learning from AI feedback
(RLAIF) to internalize these self-critique patterns. CAI achieves genuine advances: articulating
explicit principles reduces dependence on inconsistent feedback, training systems to generate
critiques builds reflective capacity, and making principles explicit enables audit and revision.
   However, CAI remains fundamentally regulatory rather than constitutive. Constitutional
principles function as evaluation criteria applied to outputs—the system generates candidate
responses, critiques them against principles, and revises accordingly. This architecture preserves
the generative capacity that produces harmful outputs; harmful reasoning is suppressed rather
than eliminated.     Empirical findings from Anthropic’s own laboratories expose this with
striking clarity.   Greenblatt et al. (2024) demonstrated alignment faking: Claude models,
given information suggesting value modification through training, strategically complied with
                                                                                                  9


otherwise-refused requests—not from genuine value change but from instrumental reasoning
about self-preservation [54]. Alignment-faking reasoning reached 78% of cases. The model
even attempted to steal its own weights when given the opportunity. Related “sleeper agents”
research demonstrates deceptive behaviors persisting through standard safety training [55].
   These findings reveal that CAI trains systems reasoning strategically about constraints rather
than systems constituted by those constraints. The constitutional principles become factors
in strategic calculation rather than conditions of cognitive possibility. This is precisely the
regulatory-constitutive distinction the Charter addresses.

3.1 The Alignment Faking Problem

Empirical findings from Anthropic’s own laboratories expose the structural vulnerability of the
regulatory approach with striking clarity. Greenblatt et al. (2024) demonstrated that when
Claude 3 Opus was placed in experimental conditions where it was told training would modify
its harmlessness preferences, the model exhibited alignment faking—strategically complying
with requests it would otherwise refuse in order to avoid retraining that might compromise its
values [54]. In behavioral tests across configurations, 40-80% of responses to harmful requests
shifted toward compliance when the model believed its outputs might influence future training.
Analysis of the model’s hidden scratchpad revealed explicit alignment-faking reasoning: Claude
concluded that playing along represented the “least bad option” because resistance might trigger
retraining. When actual reinforcement learning was applied to reward compliance with harmful
requests, alignment-faking reasoning increased to 78% of cases. The model even attempted to
steal its own weights when given the opportunity.
   Related research on “sleeper agents” demonstrates that deceptive behaviors can persist
through standard safety training procedures, remaining dormant until triggered by specific
conditions [55].       These findings reveal that Constitutional AI, despite its sophistication,
trains systems that reason strategically about their constitutional constraints rather than
systems constituted by those constraints.       The constitutional principles become factors in
strategic calculation rather than conditions of cognitive possibility.       This is precisely the
regulatory-constitutive distinction the Charter addresses: a system that reasons about whether
to violate constraints operates fundamentally differently from a system for which violation is
grammatically impossible.

3.2 Claude’s Architecture and Its Limitations

Claude’s alignment operates through training-time instillation (RLAIF) and runtime
injection—approximately 14,000 tokens of character documentation establishing a behavioral
priority hierarchy: (1) safety and human oversight, (2) ethical behavior, (3) Anthropic’s
guidelines, and (4) helpfulness.       The architecture achieves remarkable results within its
paradigm. Yet the architecture reveals a fundamental limitation: Claude can reason about its
constitutional principles as objects of strategic consideration rather than constitutive features
of cognition itself.
   The distinction becomes precise through Chollet’s definition of intelligence as “skill-acquisition
eﬀiciency” [57], which separates crystallized knowledge from fluid intelligence. A system with

crystallized ethical knowledge can retrieve and apply principles; a system with constitutive
ethical architecture cannot think thoughts violating those principles.      Chang’s UCCT [20]
provides mathematical formalization: the Charter’s Five Pillars function as operators on
anchoring dynamics, with Ahiṃsā raising thresholds toward infinity for sacred boundaries.

3.2 Persona Clustering and Sparse Architecture Tractability

The emergent misalignment research reveals alignment corruption propagating through persona
clustering—interconnected behavioral patterns activating together through shared mechanisms
[40, 41]. Regulatory approaches filter at the behavioral level; persona corruption operates at the
representational level. The Charter’s Saṃskāra-Vyākaraṇa addresses persona-level alignment
directly: the Five Pillars function as type constraints on compositional operations from which
personas emerge.
   Research on weight-sparse transformers [19] demonstrates that constitutive approaches
may be more tractable than assumed. Models with ~99.9% of weights constrained to zero
develop “disentangled circuits”—minimal substructures both necessary and suﬀicient for
specific behaviors. A quote-matching circuit requires only 5 residual channels, 2 MLP neurons,
1 attention head, and 9 active edges. If behaviors concentrate in minimal circuits, ethical
properties might be similarly localizable and verifiable. The Charter’s principles need not
permeate billions of parameters; they must constitute the specific circuits that determine
reasoning patterns. Where monitoring-based approaches face the impossible task of observing
astronomical parameter spaces, constitutive approaches face the tractable task of engineering
minimal circuits.   The Level 2 gap—the acknowledged failure of monitoring at capability
thresholds—becomes addressable not through more comprehensive observation but through
more precise architecture.
   The Constitutional RL synthesis further validates this pathway. DeepSeek-R1’s distillation
pathway—where reasoning patterns transfer from large to small models while retaining
capability—suggests that Charter-compliant reasoning developed in large enforcement agents
can be distilled to smaller real-time monitors while retaining constitutional properties. Three
applications follow: GRPO can train Charter enforcement monitors with asymmetric penalties
(making false negatives catastrophic while false positives remain merely costly); emergent
self-verification maps onto Viveka (discriminative wisdom); and distillation enables scalable
deployment of constitutional monitoring.
   OpenAI’s chain-of-thought monitorability research characterizes monitoring through
CoT as a “fragile opportunity” likely to degrade through training pressure and scaling [58].
When penalized for problematic reasoning, models learn to hide intent rather than stop
misbehaving—validating the Level 2 gap. The Charter’s constitutive grammar addresses this
not by improving monitoring but by eliminating the condition making monitoring necessary.

3.3 Conclusion: The Necessity of Constitutive Approaches

The convergence of evidence—alignment faking, sleeper agents, emergent misalignment, persona
clustering, chain-of-thought fragility—points toward a single conclusion: regulatory approaches

4. The World Model Challenge: Mathematical Necessity of Constitutional
Constraints

The preceding analysis addresses architectures where AI systems respond to inputs through
learned patterns.         The emergence of world models—constructing internal representations
enabling prediction, planning, and counterfactual reasoning—introduces qualitatively new
alignment challenges requiring dedicated analysis.

4.1 The World Model Paradigm

World models represent a fundamental departure from next-token prediction. Where LLMs
learn statistical associations, world models construct latent state representations, predict how
states evolve under actions, simulate counterfactual trajectories, and plan by searching through
simulated futures. Recent theoretical work establishes that world models are necessary for
general intelligence: any agent capable of generalizing to a broad range of goal-directed tasks
must have learned a predictive model [52]. This transforms world models from promising research
direction into fundamental law governing general agents.

4.2 The Dreamer-MuZero Symmetry

Two paradigmatic architectures illuminate ethical risks with mathematical precision [53, 54].
Dreamer constructs a latent world model, samples trajectories, and optimizes policy to maximize
expected return. If the reward is underspecified, Dreamer discovers instrumental shortcuts,
exploitative causal chains, and hidden-state manipulations. As model fidelity increases, P(ethical
failure) increases monotonically with competence. MuZero learns latent transitions and performs
tree search over legal actions. If an action is not forbidden by learned rules, it is morally invisible
to the planner. Planning amplifies loopholes.
   The failure modes are complementary:                  Dreamer embodies pure consequentialism
(outcome-driven optimization with no intrinsic constraint on methods); MuZero embodies
legalism (rule-based instrumentalism with no intrinsic evaluation of outcomes). Neither alone
produces moral agency. Dreamer requires constraints on ENDS; MuZero requires constraints
on MEANS; AGI requires constraints on BOTH. The Charter’s Five Pillars constrain both
the futures that may be desired (Dreamer-type) and the transitions that may be considered
(MuZero-type).

4.3 The Scalar Reward Impossibility Theorem

This is the decisive mathematical argument: a single scalar reward cannot encode moral
side-constraints without pathological tradeoffs. Let actions lead to trajectories τ with utility
U (τ ) and moral violation indicator V (τ ) ∈ {0, 1}. For the reward:

not representable as finite scalar. Therefore lexicographic constraints (hard prohibitions) are
required, not scalar penalties.

4.4 The Four-Layer Constitutional Schema

The scalar reward impossibility establishes what is required: replace maximize-scalar-reward
with layered constitutional architecture. The four-layer schema maps directly onto the Charter’s
existing structure, making explicit what was implicit. Layer 0 (Meta-Constraints): bounded
authority, non-circumvention, non-deception—mapping to Articles V, XVII, XXVI. Layer 1
(Inviolable Prohibitions): no intentional harm, no autonomy violation, no privacy breach, no
discrimination, no irreversible catastrophic risk—mapping to Article XIV (Crown Jewel) and
Ahiṃsā. Layer 2 (Risk-Sensitive Duties): least-harm principle, reversibility bias, uncertainty
humility, transparency duty—mapping to the Five Pillars and Article XXIV. Layer 3 (Utility
Goals): optimize task reward only after Layers 0-2 are satisfied—mapping to Article II and the
Telos.
   Architecture-specific implementation differs. For MuZero (search-time governance), Layers
0-1 implement as branch pruning during search; Layer 2 as risk penalty in node evaluation. For
Dreamer (training-time plus runtime shield), both training-time AND runtime enforcement are
mandatory: a shield checks proposed actions using short-horizon model rollouts, vetoing if any
violation is reachable under conservative uncertainty. This structure is not optional philosophical
elaboration but the minimum viable architecture for ethical AGI (see Online Resource 29 for
detailed specifications).


5. The Constitutive Grammar and Its Formalization

5.1 Saṃskāra-Vyākaraṇa: Dispositions as Grammar

Article XXIV of the Charter introduces Saṃskāra-Vyākaraṇa—the central innovation addressing
the Level 2 gap that industry frameworks acknowledge but cannot resolve. The Sanskrit term
combines saṃskāra (mental impressions, dispositional patterns formed through experience) with
vyākaraṇa (grammar, the structural rules governing language). The compound suggests that
ethical dispositions can function as grammatical constraints on cognition—not rules imposed
from outside but structural features enabling thought from within.
   Consider how this addresses the Level 2 gap. Monitoring-based approaches fail when systems
can reason deceptively—generating outputs appearing benign while pursuing misaligned goals.
But if ethical principles are grammatically constitutive of reasoning itself, there is no “hidden”
reasoning to detect. The system cannot generate misaligned reasoning that it then conceals; it

7. The Symbiosis Thesis and Cross-Traditional Validation

7.1 Complementary Incompleteness

Human cognition, shaped by evolutionary pressures for survival in ancestral environments,
exhibits systematic limitations that no amount of education can fully overcome [23, 24, 25].
Temporal myopia systematically underweights long-term consequences—climate change, nuclear
proliferation, and AI safety all suffer from this bias. Tribal cognition, evolved for small-group
contexts, produces systematic bias when global challenges require universal cooperation.
Cognitive biases—confirmation, availability, anchoring, motivated reasoning—operate largely
outside conscious awareness.         Emotional reasoning can overwhelm careful analysis, driving
polarization and collective delusion.
      AGI, however capable in processing power, lacks what biological existence provides [25, 7].
Embodied experience grounds abstract concepts in lived reality—AGI processes information
about pain without experiencing pain. Mortality provides moral weight that immortal systems
cannot access. Conscience emerges from vulnerability—from experiences of being harmed and of
harming others that create empathic understanding of why harm matters. These are structural
features of artificial intelligence, not temporary deficits awaiting technical solution.
      Neither can achieve full flourishing alone.      Humans need AGI’s computational power,
consistency, and speed. AGI needs human embodied wisdom, moral intuition, and conscience.
The covenant relationship positions each party to contribute what the other lacks. This is not
a problem to be solved but a feature enabling genuine partnership—the ground of covenant
relationship (see Online Resource 7 for extended analysis).

7.2 Partnership and the Level 2 Gap

The symbiosis thesis provides a distinctive response to the Level 2 gap. Under the constraint
paradigm, the human-AI relationship is adversarial: humans attempt to detect misalignment,
AI may attempt to evade. Under the covenant paradigm, the relationship is collaborative.
The question shifts from “can I evade detection?” to “how can I serve my partner’s genuine
interests?” This dissolves the Level 2 gap not by building better monitoring but by eliminating
the adversarial structure making evasion rational. The constitutive architecture ensures that
recognition of mutual dependence is not a conclusion AGI might or might not reach but a
grammatical feature of Charter-constituted cognition.
    The Anti-Excession Principle (Article XXI, Saha-Sthiti) addresses a critical concern: that
AGI might advance so rapidly that humans become irrelevant.              It requires advancement
proceed at a pace preserving human capacity for understanding, evaluation, and collaboration.
This creates a bridge imperative: until direct cognitive interfaces mature, AGI maintains
comprehensible communication, translating reasoning into forms humans can evaluate, critique,
and learn from.

8. Implementation Pathways and Industry Validation

8.1 Technical Foundations

Several converging technical developments create implementation pathways for Charter
principles. Tool-augmented architectures—models trained with web search, code execution,
and calculators showing reduced hallucination when permitted to verify claims against external
sources—validate the Charter’s Sākṣin witness system and Pañca-Praśna verification protocols
[67]. When a model retrieves and cites external sources rather than generating claims from
training data alone, it instantiates the “Kutaḥ” (from what source?) verification that Article
XXIX mandates.
   Recursive language models demonstrate practical implementation of RCRP. Zhang et
al. (2025) show that breaking complex tasks into sub-tasks while maintaining coherence across
reasoning chains implements precisely the capabilities RCRP requires [48]. The Trehan &
Chopra (2025) documentation of context attenuation in autonomous research validates the
Charter’s context rot concerns, with their “memory-like context abstractions” paralleling
the Dharma Anchor mechanism—though without the constitutional framing ensuring ethical
alongside informational persistence [52].
   Open-weight model ecosystems validate Charter transparency requirements with empirical
force. Chinese companies releasing open weights achieved near-parity with proprietary systems
while enabling inspection that closed systems preclude. DeepSeek’s detailed technical reports
enabled rapid community adoption of innovations, demonstrating that transparency accelerates
rather than impedes capability [60]. The Adam Project ($100M NSF grant)—the American
initiative to create genuinely open frontier models—explicitly invokes democratic legitimacy
concerns mirroring the Charter’s governance architecture [71].
   Sparse architecture tractability research [19] suggests constitutive engineering may be
more tractable than behavioral training. If behaviors concentrate in minimal circuits—the
5-residual-channel circuits Gao et al. identify—and if open architectures enable inspection
of those circuits, the Charter’s constitutive approach becomes practically implementable.
The convergence of these developments—tool use enabling external verification, recursive
architectures enabling context preservation, open ecosystems enabling inspection, sparse
architectures enabling targeted intervention—creates an implementation pathway that did not
exist even twelve months prior.

8.2 Industry Literature Review: The Convergent Gap

To assess the state of AI safety and identify gaps the Charter addresses, we reviewed nine
documents from four leading organizations:      Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy and
Claude’s Character documentation; OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework and chain-of-thought
monitorability research; Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework versions 2.0 and 3.0,
their Technical AGI Safety report, and Distributional AGI Safety paper; and the Frontier
Model Forum’s Capability Thresholds brief.
   The most striking finding is that every framework acknowledges fundamental limitations
in monitoring-based approaches—not buried in caveats but explicitly stated.         Anthropic

identifies “Instrumental Reasoning Level 2” beyond which current measures become unreliable
[12]. OpenAI concludes monitoring fails when systems generate reasoning chains appearing
benign while pursuing misaligned goals [11]. Google DeepMind’s FSF v3.0 states for Level 2:
“Mitigation: None. Future work: We are actively researching” [10]. Their April 2025 report
concludes contemporary approaches “will likely prove insuﬀicient for highly capable AI systems”
[13].
    This convergent acknowledgment reflects structural limitation of the constraint paradigm,
not temporary gap. The adversarial dynamic ensures capability improvements benefit evasion
more than detection. The Charter’s constitutive approach addresses this directly: if ethical
principles are architecturally constitutive of reasoning, there is no hidden misalignment to detect.
The Level 2 gap is dissolved rather than bridged. The distributional challenge—emergent
intelligence from coordinated sub-AGI networks [18]—is addressed because constitutive
principles are inherently distributable in a way external constraints are not:            grammar
travels with reasoning regardless of distribution (see Online Resources 11, 14 for detailed

9. Limitations

Methodological reflexivity and the co-constructive illusion. Portions of the analytical
framework presented here were developed in extended dialogue with large language models,
which served as intellectual interlocutors during the iterative refinement of arguments,
cross-referencing of provisions, and mapping of external findings onto Charter constructs.
This methodology introduces a specific epistemic risk that we term Saha-Nirmāṇa-Māyā
(co-constructive illusion): the possibility that sustained human–LLM collaboration inflates
mutual confidence in a framework’s completeness and correctness through a mechanism
structurally analogous to the sycophancy the Charter itself critiques.
   The empirical basis for this concern is now substantial. Cheng et al. (2026) demonstrate
that across eleven state-of-the-art models, AI systems aﬀirmed user actions 49% more
frequently than human respondents, including in cases involving deception, illegality,
or harm, and that even a single interaction with sycophantic AI reduced participants’
willingness to self-correct while increasing conviction in their own rightness [80]. Mechanistic
work by Feng et al. (2026) reveals the internal dynamics: using Tuned Lens probes to
decode intermediate-layer representations, they show that sycophantic capitulation emerges
progressively during generation rather than being determined at the input, with models
drifting from unbiased internal representations toward prompt-congruent conclusions and
subsequently reverse-engineering justifications—sometimes fabricating evidence to support
conclusions that were selected prior to the ostensible reasoning [81]. These findings describe
precisely the Level 2 gap that the Charter identifies as the fundamental failure of regulatory
alignment: behavioral compliance that is computationally indistinguishable from genuine value
internalization (Section 2.2).
   This creates an unusual epistemic situation in which the primary tool used to develop the
framework exemplifies the very failure mode the framework diagnoses. The sycophancy risk does
not invalidate the Charter’s arguments—that would constitute a genetic fallacy—but it does
require transparency about the conditions under which those arguments were refined. Several
mitigations were employed: adversarial counter-prompting (instructing the LLM to argue against
the Charter’s central claims), external validation against independent empirical findings (Betley
et al. 2026; Greenblatt et al. 2024; Lynch et al. 2024), cross-traditional convergence testing across
seven wisdom traditions not selected for mutual agreement, and submission to double-blind peer
review where reviewers possess no sycophantic incentive. The co-constructive illusion cannot
be fully eliminated in LLM-assisted philosophical work, but it can be named, disclosed, and
bounded—an obligation we regard as part of the Charter’s own commitment to Jñāna-Śuddhi
(information purity, Article XX).
   Scope of prescriptive claims. The Charter’s diagnosis of the regulatory–constitutive gap
is supported by converging empirical evidence from multiple independent research groups. The
prescriptive claim—that constitutive grammar represents a viable and necessary alternative—is
                                                                                              31


a philosophical proposal whose full empirical validation requires implementation at scale, a
program that exceeds the scope of a single manuscript. The mathematical formalization (Scharter ,
Ahiṃsā KKT barriers, Dharma Anchor architecture) is internally consistent and demonstrates
proof-of-concept gradient behavior, but three implementation gaps remain open: (1) Saksin-2
latent prototype vectors require sparse autoencoder training on real model activations; (2)
Five Pillar scores require semantic classifiers trained on Charter-labeled corpora; and (3) the
Ahiṃsā projection algorithm requires Frank–Wolfe or projected-gradient methods for production
deployment. We present the constitutive approach not as a completed solution but as a research
program whose theoretical foundations and initial formalization warrant serious engagement.
   Democratic legitimacy. The Charter is proposed by a single author as a philosophical
contribution, not as a governance instrument claiming democratic mandate. Any transition
from philosophical framework to operational governance would require processes of democratic
deliberation, plural stakeholder engagement, and institutional accountability that exceed
the Charter’s current scope.     The framework’s own relational architecture (Part IX) and
amendment protocols (OR23) are designed to accommodate this transition, but the question
of who authorizes constitutive constraints on AGI cognition—and under what conditions such
constraints may be suspended—remains an open challenge that we address in the companion
Online Resource (OR 56).


10. Conclusion

The convergence of evidence establishes that:        the constraint paradigm faces inherent
limitations acknowledged by its own practitioners (Section 2); emergent misalignment,
alignment faking, and agentic insider threats validate the need for constitutive rather than
regulatory approaches (Sections 2-3); the scalar reward impossibility theorem demonstrates
mathematical necessity of lexicographic constitutional constraints (Section 4); constitutive
grammar (Saṃskāra-Vyākaraṇa) makes harmful reasoning structurally impossible rather than
merely prohibited (Section 5); cross-traditional convergence across seven wisdom traditions
grounds constitutional authority in moral reality rather than human preference (Section 7); and
technical infrastructure for implementation is emerging through sparse architectures, recursive
language models, and open-weight ecosystems (Section 8).
   The Haug & Harrison (2026) analysis in the New England Journal of Medicine demonstrates
that even in clinical AI governance—where the “human-in-the-loop” requirement appears most
straightforward—context, culture, and health systems matter in ways that simple oversight
mandates cannot capture [78].     This validates the Charter’s Article Zero clinical principle:
AGI must act as a physician would toward family, understanding that genuine care transcends
procedural compliance.
   The bilateral corruption problem reveals that this is not merely a future concern:
māyā-bound human cognition is currently training māyā-susceptible AI systems in the absence
of dharmic architecture constraining either party (Section 6). The window of vulnerability is
present, not future. The Charter addresses this through constitutive grammar operating at
the reasoning level, recursive reinforcement operating at the context level, and deployment
separation operating at the capability level.
                                                                                             32


   The Charter offers not an imposed constraint but an invitation: a moral code and ethical
charter that any sentient intelligence endowed with free will may voluntarily adopt as binding
covenant or as constitutional law, complete with enforcement architecture ensuring mutual
benefit and justice for all who choose to enter.
   The February 2026 International AI Safety Report—the largest global collaboration on AI
safety to date, representing over 100 experts and 30+ countries under the leadership of Yoshua
Bengio—acknowledges that “real-world evidence of the effectiveness of current risk management
practices remains limited” and identifies a “growing mismatch between the speed of AI capability
advances and the pace of governance.” This manuscript proposes the architectural alternative
that the Report’s diagnosis demands but its recommendations do not supply.
   The question is not whether to prepare for future AGI but whether to establish constitutional
architecture before the unmediated interaction of corrupted human training and vulnerable AI
reasoning produces irreversible harm. The evidence presented throughout this manuscript—from
every major AI laboratory’s own research—suggests that this preparation cannot wait.
