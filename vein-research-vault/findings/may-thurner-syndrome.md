# May-Thurner Syndrome (Iliac Vein Compression)

## In my words
Compression of the left common iliac vein where the right common iliac *artery*
crosses over it against the spine. The chronic pulsation/compression causes
intraluminal changes (spurs/webs) and outflow obstruction — an *obstructive*
cause of venous hypertension, distinct from valvular [[venous-reflux]]. It
classically presents as left-leg swelling, or as provoked left iliofemoral DVT.

## Why it matters
It's the canonical reminder that the P axis of [[ceap-classification]] has two
arms: a limb can fail from obstruction, not reflux — and treating only superficial
reflux ([[endovenous-ablation]]) leaves the real lesion untouched. Recognition
changes management toward iliac venography/IVUS and stenting.

## Strength & caveats
Anatomy well described. The hard research questions are *prevalence vs.
significance*: iliac compression is common on imaging in asymptomatic people, so
the open problem is which degree of compression is hemodynamically meaningful.
IVUS area-reduction thresholds (~50% often cited) are debated — verify. Stenting
outcomes data is maturing; don't overstate.

## Connections
- [[chronic-venous-insufficiency]] — *mechanism-for (obstructive)*: causes venous
  hypertension without reflux.
- [[venous-reflux]] — *confounds*: coexists with reflux; the mixed limb is the trap.
- [[endovenous-ablation]] — *contradicts*: treating superficial reflux misses the
  iliac lesion — a "failed ablation" that was a missed diagnosis.
- [[ceap-classification]] — *encodes*: it is the obstructive arm of the P axis.

## Questions
- Iliac compression is common in asymptomatic people, so prevalence ≠ significance.
  Design a study that distinguishes hemodynamically meaningful compression from an
  incidental imaging finding.
- The ~50% IVUS area-reduction threshold is debated. What outcome would you anchor a
  threshold to so it predicts benefit-from-stenting rather than just degree-of-narrowing?
