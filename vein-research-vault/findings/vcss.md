# Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS)

## In my words
A **physician-rated** severity instrument for chronic venous disease. Ten
attributes — pain, varicose veins, venous oedema, skin pigmentation, inflammation,
induration, active ulcer number, ulcer size, ulcer duration, and use of compression
therapy — each graded 0–3, summed. Unlike [[ceap-classification]]'s C-class (a
*descriptive* category), VCSS is **graded and responsive to change**, so it can
track improvement after treatment.

## Why it matters
It fills CEAP's gap as a *longitudinal* outcome: CEAP tells you what class a limb is;
VCSS tells you whether it got better. That makes VCSS a workhorse primary/secondary
endpoint in venous intervention trials ([[endovenous-ablation]]). Its successor is
the [[revised-vcss]] (rVCSS).

## Strength & caveats
Validated and responsive, but physician-scored with subjective elements (pain,
inflammation, induration) and inter-rater variability; ceiling/floor effects in mild
disease. It measures *clinician-observed* severity, not the patient's experience —
hence pairing with PROs like [[vvsymq]]. Verify psychometrics.

## Connections
- [[ceap-classification]] — *complements*: graded/responsive where CEAP is descriptive.
- [[revised-vcss]] — *superseded-by*: the 2010 refinement.
- [[reflux-severity-evaluation]] — *clinical-axis-instrument*.
- [[vvsymq]] — *paired-with*: physician score vs. patient-reported symptoms.

## Questions
- VCSS mixes objective (ulcer size) and subjective (pain) items. What does that mix do
  to its reliability, and would a subscale structure serve a trial better?
- When VCSS improves but a PRO doesn't, which do you believe and why — and what does the
  discordance tell you about the treatment?
